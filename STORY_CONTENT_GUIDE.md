# FunBookies Story Content Guide

A research-based guide to creating engaging, effective decodable stories.

## What Makes a Good Story

The five essential elements (from `generate_story.py`):

| Element | Description | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **CHARACTER WANT** | Wants something specific and clear | "Pip wants a friend" not "Pip is sad" |
| **OBSTACLE** | A real problem (not manufactured drama) | Ball rolled into dark tunnel |
| **TRY-FAIL** | Genuine attempt that fails for a logical reason | Tried to reach but arms too short |
| **RESOLUTION** | Satisfying and earned | Found courage, got the ball |
| **CAUSATION** | Each event causes the next (not random scenes) | Saw ball → chased → lost it → searched → found |

**The key test:** Can you trace a causal chain from page 1 to the end? If any page could be removed without breaking the chain, it's not earning its place.

## What Makes Children's Books Resonate

Based on research from children's literature experts and analysis of master authors like Mo Willems, Eric Carle, and Dr. Seuss.

### Core Principles

| Principle | Why It Works | Source |
|-----------|--------------|--------|
| **Rule of Three** | Problem → 3 attempts → resolution creates satisfying rhythm | Classic narrative structure |
| **Child as Problem-Solver** | Protagonist solves own problem (no adult swooping in) | Mo Willems, Dr. Seuss |
| **Emotional honesty** | Big feelings (anxiety, frustration, joy) treated seriously | 2024-2025 trends |
| **Humor & Surprise** | Unexpected twists keep engagement high | Dr. Seuss "escalating sequences" |
| **Repetition with variation** | Predictable patterns build confidence | Pattern book research |
| **51% Reader Imagination** | Leave room for children to fill in meaning | Mo Willems |
| **Respect for intelligence** | Talk to children "clearly and honestly as equals" | Dr. Seuss |

### Master Author Insights

**Mo Willems**: "My job is to create 49 percent of the story and let children fill in the other 51 percent." His characters have exaggerated expressions and simple designs that any child could draw. The Pigeon works because the reader IS the kid saying no.

**Eric Carle**: Bold colors, collage textures, white space for imagination. "The caterpillar is a symbol of hope—tiny and insignificant but grows to be a beautiful butterfly."

**Dr. Seuss**: Constrained vocabulary forced creativity. "Kids can see a moral coming a mile off"—let themes emerge naturally. Escalating sequences: start with normalcy, multiply action until chaos, resolve.

### Themes That Resonate (2024-2025)

1. **Emotional Literacy** - Managing big feelings (anxiety, anger, disappointment)
2. **Neurodiversity** - Characters who think/learn differently as protagonists
3. **Environmental Stewardship** - Conservation, gardening, animal habitats
4. **Everyday Diversity** - Representation as normal, not the point of the story
5. **STEM Integration** - Science woven naturally into adventures

## Word Categories in Decodable Books

Each FunBookies book uses three categories of words:

### The Word Palette Philosophy

**CRITICAL:** Treat word lists as inspiration, not constraint.

```
If a phonics word doesn't fit naturally, DON'T USE IT.
```

A clear story with slightly fewer target words beats a confusing story that hits every phonics pattern.

### 1. Sound-Out Words (Decodable)
Words that use ONLY the phonics patterns taught at or before this level. These are the core of the text.

**Example at B2 (CVC all short vowels):**
- ✅ cat, sat, hot, mud, bed, got, run
- ❌ cake (magic e), train (vowel team)

### Vocabulary Substitutions for Early Levels

Some words are too complex even if technically decodable. Use simpler alternatives:

| Too Hard | Use Instead | Why |
|----------|-------------|-----|
| washes | gets, rubs | Multi-syllable, complex ending |
| ready | set | Abstract, harder to decode |
| water | (ok as sight word) | Keep sentences short around it |
| towel | (ok) | Keep it simple |
| beautiful | pretty, nice | Too many syllables |
| because | so | Simpler connector |

### 2. Sight Words (Heart Words)
High-frequency words that may not be fully decodable but are essential for natural sentences. Keep limited (5-10 per book).

**Common sight words:** the, a, is, was, said, to, I, he, she, we, they, you, are, have, do, what, there

### 3. Reach Words (High-Interest Vocabulary)

**Definition:** Words that are BEYOND the current phonics level but are:
- Concrete and picturable
- High-interest to children
- Fully supported by illustrations
- Stored as sight words through repeated exposure

**The key distinction:**
| Type | Example | Problem? |
|------|---------|----------|
| Decodable word guessed from picture | "cat" guessed instead of decoded | ❌ Yes - undermines phonics |
| Reach word with image support | "trampoline" with clear picture | ✅ No - motivating vocabulary |
| Abstract reach word | "adventure" with no visual anchor | ⚠️ Risky - hard to retain |

### What Makes a Good Reach Word

1. **Concrete & picturable** - One clear visual referent
2. **High kid-interest** - Things children already talk about
3. **Phonetically complex but visually simple** - Easy to recognize
4. **Limited per book** - 1-3 reach words max
5. **Repeated across pages** - Builds familiarity

### Reach Word Categories

| Category | Examples | Why Kids Love Them |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Vehicles** | airplane, helicopter, submarine, motorcycle | Movement, power, adventure |
| **Animals** | elephant, crocodile, flamingo, butterfly | Fascination with creatures |
| **Dinosaurs** | tyrannosaurus, triceratops, pterodactyl | Endless kid obsession |
| **Space** | astronaut, planet, rocket, satellite | Wonder, exploration |
| **Big actions** | trampoline, somersault, avalanche | Physical, exciting |
| **Food** | spaghetti, chocolate, strawberry, popsicle | Immediate connection |
| **Weather** | thunderstorm, rainbow, tornado, blizzard | Drama, sensory |

### Reach Word Ratio by Band

| Band | Pages | Reach Words | Ratio |
|------|-------|-------------|-------|
| A0-A2 | 8-10 | 1-2 | ~1 per 5 pages |
| A3-B2 | 10-12 | 2-3 | ~1 per 4 pages |
| B3-B6 | 12-16 | 3-4 | ~1 per 4 pages |
| B7+ | 16-20 | 4-6 | ~1 per 3-4 pages |

### Example: Adding Reach Words to B2

Instead of:
> "The cat sat on the mat."

With reach words:
> "The cat sat on the mat. Then—WHOOSH! A helicopter! The cat hid."

- **Decodable**: cat, sat, on, the, mat, then, hid
- **Reach word**: helicopter (pictured clearly)
- **Effect**: Stakes rise, vocabulary expands, decoding stays intact

## Story Structures by Band

### Bands A-B: Short, Pattern-Based

```
Page 1-2: Introduce character + desire
Page 3-4: First attempt (fails)
Page 5-6: Second attempt (fails differently)
Page 7-8: Third attempt / twist (succeeds!)
Page 9-10: Resolution + emotional payoff
```

### Bands C-D: Longer, Character-Driven

```
Chapter 1: Status quo + inciting incident
Chapter 2: First attempt + complication
Chapter 3: Midpoint revelation/change
Chapter 4: Darkest moment (all seems lost)
Chapter 5: Climax + resolution
Chapter 6: New normal + hint at next adventure
```

## Scene Description Requirements for Image Generation

**CRITICAL**: AI image generators will FILL IN any unspecified details—often incorrectly! Every scene description must be explicit about:

### 1. Prop Tracking
If a prop moves to a specific location, **EVERY subsequent scene** must explicitly state where it is until retrieval:

| Page | Text | Bad Scene | Good Scene |
|------|------|-----------|------------|
| 4 | "Ball rolled into tunnel" | "Rex at tunnel entrance" | "Rex at tunnel entrance. Ball visible as small red dot INSIDE the dark tunnel." |
| 5 | "Rex was scared" | "Close-up of Rex's scared face" | "Close-up of Rex's scared face. Dark tunnel behind him, ball visible INSIDE." |
| 6 | "Rosie encouraged Rex" | "Rex and Rosie by tunnel" | "Rex and Rosie by tunnel entrance. Ball still visible INSIDE dark tunnel." |

### 2. Character Exclusion
If a character is ALONE, explicitly state who is NOT present:

| Situation | Bad | Good |
|-----------|-----|------|
| Solo moment | "Rex entering tunnel" | "Rex entering tunnel ALONE. Rosie NOT in scene." |
| First meeting | "Dog sees rabbit" | "ONLY dog visible. Rabbit enters from right side." |

### 3. Emotional Continuity
Each scene must show the CURRENT emotional state:

| Emotion | Physical Description |
|---------|---------------------|
| Scared | "ears flattened, eyes wide, tail tucked, body low, trembling slightly" |
| Brave | "ears forward, eyes focused, chin up, chest out, stepping confidently" |
| Happy | "ears perked, eyes bright, tail wagging high, mouth open in pant-smile" |
| Sad | "ears drooping, eyes downcast, tail hanging, shoulders slumped" |

### 4. Template Text to Avoid

| Don't Use | Why | Better Alternative |
|-----------|-----|-------------------|
| "[X] and [Y] were pals. They had fun in the sun!" | Generic, applies to any story | Start with specific action or dialogue |
| "One sunny day..." | Overused | Jump into the scene |
| "Once upon a time..." | Wrong register for decodables | Present-tense action |
| "The end" | Missed opportunity | Echo the opening, show transformation |

## Logic and Continuity Rules

**CRITICAL:** Every sentence must make logical sense. Check cause and effect. Check physical reality.

### Logic Errors to Avoid

| Bad | Problem | Good Alternative |
|-----|---------|------------------|
| "He got wet in the sun." | Sun doesn't make you wet | "He got wet in the rain." |
| "Now Max is not wet." (after a bath) | He'd be soaking wet! | "Mom dried Max with a towel." |
| "He is wet and red." | Why red? Makes no sense | "He is wet and muddy." |
| "The cat ran to sit." | Awkward phrasing | "The cat ran. Then it sat." |

### Good Logic Examples

| Cause | Effect | Why It Works |
|-------|--------|--------------|
| "Max jumped in the mud." | "Mud splashed on his nose!" | Clear cause → effect |
| "Mom dried Max with a towel." | "Now his fur was fluffy." | Action → result |
| "The pup ran fast." | "He wanted that ball!" | Motivation → action |

### Continuity Checklist

Before writing each page, ask:
1. **State:** What state is the character in from the previous page?
2. **Logic:** What would logically happen next?
3. **Flow:** Does this follow from what just happened?

### Scene Continuity Examples

| Page | Text | Scene Must Show |
|------|------|-----------------|
| Page 4 | "He fell in the mud" | Character NOW muddy |
| Page 5 | "Mom called him inside" | Character STILL muddy |
| Page 6 | "Time for a bath!" | Character STILL muddy, near bathtub |
| Page 7 | "Splash! Splash!" | Character IN water, mud washing off |
| Page 8 | "All clean now!" | Character WET but clean |

## What to Avoid

| Don't | Why | Do Instead |
|-------|-----|------------|
| Adult saves the day | Undermines child agency | Child protagonist solves own problem |
| Heavy-handed moral | Kids see it coming | Let theme emerge naturally |
| Talking down | Children sense condescension | Treat readers as intelligent equals |
| Only diversity books about being diverse | Tokenizing | Diverse characters in universal stories |
| Guessing decodable words from pictures | Undermines phonics | Use pictures for reach words only |
| Random echo/refrain words | No emotional payoff | Echo words that carry meaning |
| Vague emotions | Young readers need clarity | Name the emotion on every page |
| Ending doesn't match setup | Unsatisfying | Mirror the opening problem |
| Scene without prop location | Image generator fills in wrong | Always state where key props are |
| Ambiguous character presence | Generator might include wrong chars | Explicitly include/exclude characters |
| Logic errors | Breaks immersion | Check cause → effect on every page |
| Discontinuous states | Confuses reader | Track character state page to page |
